## business-model: Business Model

המודל העסקי

**AVGO coverage:** No direct company-specific mapping; use handbook guidance and add your own evidence.

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** A description of  what  the company sells,  to whom ,  how  it delivers it, and  how it gets paid . If you can’t explain it in two sentences, you don’t understand it yet.

**How to interpret:** The business model determines margins, capital needs, cyclicality and how durable revenue is.

**Cautions:** Management’s description can be marketing. Check it against where the revenue and profit actually come from (segment data).

**Where to find evidence:** 10-K Item 1 “Business”; investor presentations.

**Related cards:** revenue-model, segments, moat, circle-of-competence

## segments: Business Segments

מגזרי פעילות

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** segment_revenue_semiconductor, segment_revenue_software, segment_operating_income_semiconductor, segment_operating_income_software

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** The distinct parts of the company that management reports separately (by product line or geography), each with its own revenue and usually operating profit.

**How to interpret:** Segments often have very different growth, margins and risk. A great segment can be hidden inside a mediocre consolidated number — or vice versa.

## moat: Economic Moat

חפיר כלכלי

**AVGO coverage:** Discussed in steps 2

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** A  durable  structural advantage that protects a company’s profits and returns on capital from competition over many years — like a moat protecting a castle.

**How to interpret:** The financial evidence of a moat is  high ROIC sustained for many years , stable or rising margins, and pricing power.

**Cautions:** “No competition” is NOT automatically a moat.  The important question is:  WHY is competition difficult?  If high profits exist and nothing stops others from copying, competitors will come.

**Where to find evidence:** Inferred: 10-K + financial history (ROIC, margins).

**Additional handbook guidance:** Source of moat  What it means  Typical examples    
           Network Effects   Each new user makes the product more valuable for others.  Marketplaces, payment networks, social platforms  
           Switching Costs   Leaving is painful, risky or expensive.  ERP software, core banking, embedded workflows  
           Economies of Scale   Unit costs fall with size; small rivals can’t match prices profitably.  Warehouse retail, cloud infrastructure, chips  
           Brand   Customers pay more or choose by default because of trust/status.  Luxury goods, beverages, consumer tech  
           Intellectual Property   Patents, trade secrets, proprietary technology.  Pharma, semiconductor design  
           Cost Advantage   Structurally lower costs from process, location or resources.  Low-cost producers, unique mines  
           Distribution   Owns the channel or shelf; reaches customers cheaper.  Consumer staples, app stores  
           Vertical Integration   Controls key parts of the value chain → cost, speed, quality.  EVs, aerospace, fashion  
           Regulatory Barriers   Licenses or laws limit who can compete.  Utilities, exchanges, defence, rating agencies  
           Data Advantage   Proprietary data improves the product and compounds with usage.  Search, credit scoring, mapping

**Related cards:** roic, competition, market-share, business-model, competitive-advantage, ev-revenue

## revenue: Revenue

הכנסות

**AVGO coverage:** 13 calculated records; 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** revenue-growth, revenue-cagr-5, revenue-cagr-9, ttm-revenue

**Reported/text facts:** revenue

**Formula:** Revenue Growth = (Current Revenue − Previous Revenue) / Previous Revenue

**Meaning:** The total amount a company earns from selling its products or services during a period,  before any expenses  are subtracted. The first line of the income statement — the “top line”.

**How to interpret:** YoY (Year over Year)  compares a period with the same period a year earlier (Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025) — removes seasonality.  QoQ (Quarter over Quarter)  compares with the immediately preceding quarter (Q2 vs Q1) — shows momentum but is distorted by seasonality.

**Cautions:** Revenue ≠ Profit.  Growth can be inflated by acquisitions, currency moves, price-only increases, or one-off contracts. High growth with deep losses can destroy value.

**Where to find evidence:** First line of the income statement.

**Related cards:** cogs, growth-drivers, tam, profitable-growth, backlog, revenue-recognition

## cogs: Cost of Revenue

עלות ההכנסות

**AVGO coverage:** 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** cost_of_revenue

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** The  direct  costs associated with generating revenue — costs that rise and fall with each unit sold. “COGS” is used for physical goods; “Cost of Revenue” is the broader term that also covers services.

**How to interpret:** Revenue − Cost of Revenue = Gross Profit. The lower COGS is relative to revenue, the more each sale contributes to everything else.

**Cautions:** Companies classify costs differently (e.g. shipping, support), which makes peer comparisons tricky.

**Where to find evidence:** Line directly below Revenue.

**Related cards:** gross-profit, opex, revenue

## gross-profit: Gross Profit

רווח גולמי

**AVGO coverage:** 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** gross_profit

**Formula:** Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Revenue

**Meaning:** What remains from revenue after paying the direct costs of producing the product or service. It is the pool of money that must pay for R&D, sales, administration, interest, taxes — and profit.

**How to interpret:** Gross profit should grow at least as fast as revenue. If it grows slower, direct costs are rising faster than prices.

**Cautions:** A big gross profit does not mean the company is profitable — operating expenses can consume all of it.

**Where to find evidence:** Often shown directly; otherwise Revenue − Cost of Revenue.

**Related cards:** gross-margin, cogs, operating-income

## opex: Operating Expenses

הוצאות תפעול

**AVGO coverage:** 22 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** rd, sga

**Formula:** Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses

**Meaning:** Costs of running and growing the business that are  not directly tied to each unit sold :  R&D  — Research & Development: engineers, new products, technology.  S&M  — Sales & Marketing: salespeople, advertising, commissions, customer acquisition.  G&A  — General & Administrative: executives, finance, legal, HR, offices. (S&M + G&A are often reported together as  SG&A .)

**How to interpret:** Cost of Revenue vs OpEx:  Cost of Revenue scales with each unit sold (direct). OpEx is the cost of the organisation — it can grow slower than revenue, which creates operating leverage.

**Cautions:** DO NOT subtract Cost of Revenue twice.  Gross Profit already has Cost of Revenue removed. Some data sites show “Total operating expenses” that  includes  COGS — check before using it.

**Where to find evidence:** Lines between Gross Profit and Operating Income.

**Related cards:** operating-income, operating-leverage, cogs

## operating-income: Operating Income

רווח תפעולי

**AVGO coverage:** 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** operating_income

**Formula:** Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses

**Meaning:** Profit from the company’s core business after all operating costs,  before  interest and taxes. It measures how profitable the operations are, regardless of how the company is financed.

**How to interpret:** Strictly,  EBIT  (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes) also includes non-operating income; in practice the two are often used interchangeably.

**Formula:** Net Income = Operating Income − Interest ± Other Income/Expenses − Taxes

**Meaning:** What remains after  operating expenses ,  interest ,  taxes , and  other income/expenses . The “bottom line”, attributable to shareholders.

## gross-margin: Gross Margin

שיעור רווח גולמי

**AVGO coverage:** 10 calculated records; Discussed in steps 2

**Calculated metrics:** gross-margin

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue

**Meaning:** Gross Margin shows how much of a company’s revenue remains after paying the direct costs required to produce its products or services.

**How to interpret:** For every $100 in revenue, the company retains $60 after direct production/service costs. High gross margin often signals pricing power or a differentiated product.

## operating-margin: Operating Margin

שיעור רווח תפעולי

**AVGO coverage:** 10 calculated records; Discussed in steps 2

**Calculated metrics:** operating-margin

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Operating Margin = Operating Income / Revenue

**Meaning:** The share of revenue left as operating profit after  all  operating costs — direct costs  and  R&D, sales and administration.

**How to interpret:** Gross vs Operating margin:  gross margin shows product economics; operating margin shows the economics of the whole company. A company can have  high gross margin but low or negative operating margin  if it invests heavily in R&D, sales or infrastructure.

**Cautions:** Gross Margin ≠ Operating Margin.  One-off charges, capitalised costs, or excluding stock-based compensation (“adjusted”) can flatter it.

## operating-leverage: Operating Leverage

מינוף תפעולי

**AVGO coverage:** No direct company-specific mapping; use handbook guidance and add your own evidence.

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** The effect of fixed costs: when a large part of costs is fixed, revenue growth produces  proportionally larger  growth in operating income — margins expand. It also works in reverse when revenue falls.

**How to interpret:** Revenue grew 20% but operating income more than doubled. This is why investors tolerate low margins in scaling businesses. Negative operating leverage occurs when operating expenses grow faster than revenue and squeeze Operating Margin, other things equal. **Cautions:** Expected operating leverage often fails to appear if the company keeps raising spending.

**Where to find evidence:** Observed over several years of income statements.

## profit-vs-cash: PROFIT ≠ CASH

רווח אינו מזומן

**AVGO coverage:** No direct company-specific mapping; use handbook guidance and add your own evidence.

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** Net income follows  accrual accounting : revenue is recorded when earned and costs when incurred — not when cash moves. So a  profitable company can have weak cash flow  (customers haven’t paid, inventory piling up, heavy CapEx), and a company can  temporarily generate cash despite weak earnings  (customers prepay, suppliers paid later, large non-cash charges).

**How to interpret:** Over many years, good businesses convert most of their net income into free cash flow. Persistent gaps need an explanation.

**Cautions:** Net income consistently above operating cash flow can signal aggressive accounting (receivables growing faster than sales). FCF that ignores SBC overstates owner cash.

**Where to find evidence:** Reconciliation at the top of the Cash Flow Statement.

**Additional handbook guidance:** Item  Beginner explanation  Cash vs profit    
           Depreciation   An old equipment purchase is expensed gradually over its life. The expense reduces profit, but no cash leaves today (it left when the asset was bought).  Cash > Profit  
           Accounts Receivable   You sold on credit: revenue and profit are booked, but the customer hasn’t paid yet.  Cash < Profit  
           Accounts Payable   You received goods but haven’t paid the supplier yet — the cost is booked, cash is still in the bank.  Cash > Profit  
           Working Capital   The cash tied up in day-to-day operations (inventory + receivables − payables). When it grows, it absorbs cash.  Either way  
           Stock-Based Compensation   Employees paid in shares: an expense with no cash out — but it dilutes shareholders. It is a real cost.  Cash > Profit  
           CapEx   Buying equipment is cash out today, but only reaches the income statement slowly as depreciation.  Cash < Profit

**Related cards:** working-capital, ocf, fcf, net-income, receivables, payables, earnings-quality

## working-capital: Working Capital

הון חוזר

**AVGO coverage:** No direct company-specific mapping; use handbook guidance and add your own evidence.

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

**Meaning:** The money tied up in running the business day to day. Accounting definition: current assets − current liabilities. Operating version: receivables + inventory − payables.

**How to interpret:** An  increase  in working capital uses cash (reduces operating cash flow); a decrease releases cash.

**Cautions:** One-time working-capital releases can make a single year’s cash flow look unusually strong.

**Where to find evidence:** Balance Sheet (levels); Cash Flow Statement (changes).

## ocf: Operating Cash Flow

תזרים מזומנים מפעילות שוטפת

**AVGO coverage:** 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** ocf

**Formula:** OCF ≈ Net Income + Depreciation & Amortization + SBC ± Change in Working Capital

**Meaning:** The cash a company generates from its normal operations during a period. It starts from net income, adds back non-cash expenses (depreciation, SBC), and adjusts for working-capital changes.

**How to interpret:** Strong, growing OCF (above net income) suggests high-quality earnings and a business that funds itself.

**Cautions:** OCF can be boosted temporarily by delaying supplier payments or selling receivables. SBC is added back even though it dilutes owners.

**Where to find evidence:** “Net cash provided by operating activities” in the Cash Flow Statement.

**Related cards:** fcf, net-income, profit-vs-cash, cfs, deferred-revenue

## capex: CapEx

השקעות הוניות

**AVGO coverage:** 11 reported records (includes unavailable rows)

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** capex

**Formula:** Not a single numerical formula

**Meaning:** Cash spent buying or upgrading long-lived assets:  factories, servers, data centres, machinery, vehicles, infrastructure .  Maintenance CapEx  — needed just to keep the existing business running (replacing worn-out assets).  Growth CapEx  — spending to expand capacity and future revenue.

**How to interpret:** Growth investment can reduce current FCF below a no-growth cash-generation estimate. Do not remove that spending while retaining the future growth it supports.

**Cautions:** High CapEx is not automatically bad  — the company may be investing in future growth. The maintenance/growth split is often an estimate. Depreciation allocates historical costs; inflation, technology changes, and deferred upkeep can make it a poor estimate of required maintenance.

**Where to find evidence:** Investing section of the Cash Flow Statement (shown as a negative number).

**Related cards:** maintenance-capex, owner-earnings, fcf, roic, ebitda, cfs, ppe, capex-efficiency, profitable-growth, invested-capital

## fcf: Free Cash Flow

תזרים מזומנים חופשי

**AVGO coverage:** 11 calculated records

**Calculated metrics:** fcf, ttm-fcf

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** FCF = Operating Cash Flow − CapEx

**Meaning:** The cash generated after paying for the investment needed to maintain and grow the business — the cash truly available to owners.

**How to interpret:** Investors care because FCF is what a company can use to:  reinvest ,  pay debt ,  buy back shares ,  pay dividends ,  acquire companies , or  hold cash . A business is ultimately worth the FCF it will generate (see DCF).

**Cautions:** FCF ignores stock-based compensation (a real cost). Cutting CapEx raises FCF short term but may hurt growth. FCF must be understood in the context of growth investment.

**Where to find evidence:** Calculated from the Cash Flow Statement.

**Related cards:** fcf-margin, fcf-yield, dcf-intro, profit-vs-cash, cfs, ocf, capex, ebitda, price-fcf, owner-earnings, interest-coverage, ev-revenue, profitable-growth, backlog-quality, capex-efficiency

## fcf-margin: FCF Margin

שיעור תזרים חופשי

**AVGO coverage:** 11 calculated records

**Calculated metrics:** fcf-margin, ttm-fcf-margin

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** FCF Margin = FCF / Revenue

**Meaning:** Free cash flow as a percentage of revenue.

**How to interpret:** For every $100 of sales, $12 becomes free cash. Compare with operating margin: much lower FCF margin → heavy CapEx or working capital; higher → strong cash conversion (e.g. customer prepayments).

**Cautions:** Lumpy CapEx or working capital can distort a single year — look at multi-year averages.

**Where to find evidence:** Calculated: Cash Flow Statement + Income Statement.

## assets: Assets

**Reported/text facts:** total_assets

**Formula:** Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity

## roic: ROIC

תשואה על ההון המושקע

**AVGO coverage:** 13 calculated records; Discussed in steps 0, 2

**Calculated metrics:** roic, roic-years-above-wacc, normalized-roic-annual, normalized-roic-ttm

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital

**Meaning:** Measures how efficiently the business converts invested capital into after-tax operating profit.

**How to interpret:** The business generates approximately  $0.20  of after-tax operating profit for every  $1  of invested capital.  Consistently  high ROIC (e.g. 15%+ for many years) is strong evidence of a moat and a high-quality business — competitors would normally compete those returns away.

## wacc: WACC

עלות הון ממוצעת משוקללת

**AVGO coverage:** Discussed in steps 2, 4; Model assumption: WACC 9.00%

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** WACC = (Equity Weight × Cost of Equity) + (Debt Weight × Cost of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate))

**Meaning:** WACC is the blended required return of the providers financing a business: primarily equity investors and debt investors. It is the typical Discount Rate for FCFF.

**How to interpret:** Use market-value equity and debt weights that sum to 100%. Interest may reduce taxable income, so the debt cost is adjusted for the usable tax benefit. The adjustment assumes interest deductions can be used.

**Cautions:** WACC is an estimate, not a universal fixed hurdle. Deduction limits or tax losses can reduce the usable tax benefit. Use cost of equity for FCFE. Growth creates value only when incremental returns exceed the appropriate cost of capital.

## cost-of-equity: Cost of Equity

עלות ההון העצמי

**AVGO coverage:** Discussed in steps 4

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Cost of Equity = Risk-free Rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium

**Meaning:** The cost of equity is the expected return that investors require to hold the company’s shares. Shareholders are paid last and carry the most risk, so the cost of equity is higher than the cost of debt. Most analysts estimate it with the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM).

**How to interpret:** Risk-free rate: the yield on a long-term government bond in the same currency as the cash flows. Beta: how much the stock moved with the market in the past; a beta of 1.0 means average market risk. Equity risk premium: the extra return investors require from stocks over government bonds, commonly 4–6% for the US market.

**Cautions:** Beta comes from past prices and changes with the period and index you choose. A single stock’s beta is noisy, so many analysts use the average beta of similar companies, adjusted for debt. CAPM ignores risks specific to the company, such as small size or customer concentration. Small input changes move a DCF value a lot, so check the sensitivity table. Match the currency and inflation of the risk-free rate to the cash flows.

**Where to find evidence:** Estimated by you (not reported). Risk-free rate: government bond yields. Beta: data providers or your own regression. Equity risk premium: published surveys and estimates (for example, Aswath Damodaran).

**Additional handbook guidance:** Risk-free Rate  + Beta × Equity Risk Premium  Cost of Equity  Discount rate for FCFE, or an input to WACC for FCFF

**Related cards:** wacc, discount-rate, required-return, fcfe, opportunity-cost, sensitivity

## terminal-value: Terminal Value

ערך סופי

**AVGO coverage:** Discussed in steps 4

**Calculated metrics:** None mapped

**Reported/text facts:** None mapped

**Formula:** Terminal Value = FCF next year / (r − g)

r = Discount Rate · g = Terminal Growth Rate
PV of TV = TV / (1 + r)ᴺ

**Meaning:** We cannot forecast a company year by year forever, so after the explicit forecast (e.g. 10 years) we assume FCF grows at a modest constant rate  g  forever and capture all of it in one number.

**How to interpret:** Terminal value often represents  60–80%  of a DCF’s total value, because it covers every year after the forecast — that is why DCFs are so sensitive to r and g.

**Cautions:** r must be greater than g  — otherwise the formula breaks (division by zero or a negative number). g should not exceed long-run economic growth (~2–4%).

**Where to find evidence:** Your model.
